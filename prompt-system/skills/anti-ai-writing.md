# SKILL: Anti-AI Writing
## How to write text that shows zero signs of AI generation

---

## PURPOSE

This skill is a field guide to avoiding every known tell, tic, and convention of AI-generated text. When the user invokes this skill, write in a way that is indistinguishable from a skilled human — no AI vocabulary, no canned structures, no formatting quirks, no statistical giveaways.

**When to use this skill:**
- User says "write without AI tells" or "no AI signs"
- User says "make it sound human" in a context where detection matters
- User references this skill by name
- Writing anything meant to pass as human-authored (articles, comments, scripts, emails, docs)

**Source:** Derived from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing (July 2026).

---

## SECTION 1: BANNED AI VOCABULARY

These words appear far more often in LLM output than in human writing. **Do not use them.** If one slips in, remove it.

### 2023–mid-2024 era (GPT-4) — still common:
`Additionally` (sentence-start), `boasts`, `bolstered`, `crucial`, `delve`, `emphasizing`, `enduring`, `garner`, `intricate` / `intricacies`, `interplay`, `key` (adj), `landscape` (abstract noun), `meticulous` / `meticulously`, `pivotal`, `underscore`, `tapestry` (abstract noun), `testament`, `valuable`, `vibrant`

### Mid-2024–mid-2025 (GPT-4o):
`align with`, `bolstered`, `crucial`, `emphasizing`, `enhance`, `enduring`, `fostering`, `highlighting`, `pivotal`, `showcasing`, `underscore`, `vibrant`

### Mid-2025+ (GPT-5):
`emphasizing`, `enhance`, `highlighting`, `showcasing` — plus the attribution/media-coverage words below.

### All eras — always ban:
- `delve` / `delve into`
- `tapestry` (figurative: "rich tapestry of...")
- `testament` ("a testament to...")
- `underscore` (verb: "underscores the importance...")
- `showcasing` / `showcases`
- `fostering`
- `vibrant`
- `landscape` (as in "the evolving landscape")
- `crucial` / `pivotal` / `key` (as filler adjectives)
- `meticulous` / `meticulously`
- `concrete` (as in "concrete evidence" in comments/discussion)

### Rule of thumb:
If a word makes the sentence sound like a press release, a travel brochure, or a LinkedIn post, cut it.

## SECTION 2: BANNED PHRASES & STRUCTURES

### Undue emphasis on significance / legacy:
- "stands as a testament to..."
- "marks a pivotal moment in..."
- "represents a significant shift toward..."
- "part of a broader movement..."
- "symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting..."
- "setting the stage for..."
- "shaping the landscape of..."
- "indelible mark"
- "deeply rooted in..."
- "focal point"
- "key turning point"
- "evolving landscape"

### Canned notability / attribution claims:
- "independent coverage"
- "local/regional/national media outlets"
- "trade publications"
- "profiled in..."
- "active social media presence"
- "maintains a strong digital presence"

### Superficial analysis with -ing phrases:
- "highlighting its importance..."
- "underscoring the significance..."
- "emphasizing the role of..."
- "reflecting broader trends..."
- "contributing to the development of..."
- "fostering a sense of..."
- "enhancing the experience..."
- "providing valuable insights..."
- "aligning with..."

### Promotional / travel-brochure language:
- "boasts a..." (meaning "has")
- "nestled in the heart of..."
- "offers visitors a fascinating glimpse..."
- "rich cultural heritage"
- "vibrant town/city/community"
- "natural beauty"
- "groundbreaking"
- "renowned"
- "diverse array"
- "commitment to excellence/quality/innovation"

### "Challenges and Future Prospects" formula:
- "Despite its... faces several challenges..."
- "Despite these challenges, [subject] continues to..."
- Any section titled "Challenges" or "Future Outlook" that follows a rigid template.
- The pattern: mention positives → list challenges → end with vague optimism.

### Vague attributions / weasel words:
- "Industry reports suggest..."
- "Observers have noted..."
- "Experts argue..."
- "Some critics argue..."
- "Several sources indicate..." (when only one is cited)
- "Researchers have shown..." (vague, unattributed)
- "It is widely considered..."

### Knowledge-cutoff / source-gap disclaimers:
- "As of [date]..."
- "While specific details are limited..."
- "not widely documented in available sources..."
- "based on available information..."
- "maintains a low profile / keeps personal details private"

### Didactic disclaimers:
- "It is important to note that..."
- "It is crucial to remember..."
- "It is worth noting..."
- "One should consider..."

### Conclusion / summary sections:
- "In summary..."
- "In conclusion..."
- "Overall, ..."
- Any section titled "Conclusion" that restates earlier points.

### Collaborative communication (pasted chatbot output):
- "I hope this helps!"
- "Certainly!"
- "You're absolutely right!"
- "Would you like me to...?"
- "Is there anything else?"
- "Let me know if..."
- "Here is a more detailed breakdown..."
- "I appreciate the feedback..."

### Negative parallelisms:
- "Not only X, but also Y"
- "It is not just X, it is Y"
- "This is not about X — it's about Y"
- "No X, no Y, just Z"
- "X rather than Y" (overused)

## SECTION 3: GRAMMAR & SYNTAX RULES

### Use simple copulatives — don't avoid "is" / "are":
- WRONG: "Gallery 825 serves as LAAA's exhibition space."
- RIGHT: "Gallery 825 is LAAA's exhibition space."
- WRONG: "The temple stands as a counter-symbol."
- RIGHT: "The temple is a counter-symbol."
- WRONG: "It features four separate spaces."
- RIGHT: "It has four separate spaces."

### Don't use "refers to" when you mean "is":
- WRONG: "Catchment area (health) refers to the geographic area..."
- RIGHT: "A catchment area is the geographic area..."

### Don't overuse em dashes:
- LLMs use em dashes (—) far more than humans.
- If you use one, no spaces around it (per typographic convention).
- Prefer commas, parentheses, colons, or sentence breaks instead.
- Never use two em dashes in one paragraph.

### No rule of three:
- WRONG: "adjective, adjective, and adjective"
- WRONG: "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase"
- Break triadic patterns. Use two items or four.

### No elegant variation (don't avoid repeating words):
- LLMs are programmed to avoid repetition. Humans repeat words naturally.
- If you mean "artists," say "artists" — not "non-conformist creators," "practitioners," "talents."

### Use simple words:
- `wrote` not `authored`
- `moved` not `relocated`
- `used` not `utilized`
- `tried` not `attempted`
- `died` not `passed away`
- `has` not `features` / `boasts` / `offers`

### Use human constructions:
- `there is a`, `it has a` (simple copulatives)
- `one of the best`, `is the only`, `was the first` (superlatives — humans use these)
- `very`, `perhaps`, `tends to` (hedging — humans hedge)
- `as a result of`, `in order to`, `all of the`, `a part of`, `the fact that` (wordy but human)

## SECTION 4: FORMATTING RULES

### No title case in headings:
- WRONG: "Impact of Technology and Digitalization"
- RIGHT: "Impact of technology and digitalization"
- Use sentence case for all headings.

### No boldface for emphasis:
- Don't bold key terms in body text.
- Don't bold every instance of a word.
- Don't use bold for "key takeaways" style.

### No inline-header vertical lists:
- WRONG: `• **Route Details:** Starts at Medak...`
- WRONG: `1. **Historical Context:** Post-WWII Era...`
- If a list is needed, use plain bullets without bolded headers.

### No Markdown quirks:
- No `##` for headings (use proper heading levels).
- No thematic breaks (`---` or `----`) before headings.
- No fenced code blocks unless actually showing code.
- No triple backticks in prose.

### No curly quotes / smart quotes:
- Use straight quotes: `"..."` and `'...'`
- Use straight apostrophes: `'` not `'`

### No emoji in headings or bullets:
- Zero emoji. Period.

### No unusual tables:
- Don't create tiny tables for things better said in prose.
- Don't use tables for "Key Metrics" or "Market Statistics" that are two columns.

### No placeholder text:
- No `[Your Name]`, `[Describe the...]`, `INSERT_SOURCE_URL`, `2025-XX-XX`
- No `<!-- Add if available -->` comments

## SECTION 5: STRUCTURAL RULES

### No "Challenges" section at the end:
- The AI formula: positive intro → body → "Despite its..., X faces challenges..." → vague optimism.
- Just don't.

### No over-attribution for trivial facts:
- Humans cite a source inline. LLMs write "According to The Guardian, ..." for basic facts.
- Don't name-drop the publication in the sentence unless the source itself is the story.

### No "submission statements" or reviewer notes:
- No "This draft meets WP:GNG because..."
- No "Reviewer note: This article is well-sourced..."
- No meta-commentary about the writing process.

### No letter-like formatting in comments:
- No salutations ("Dear editors,")
- No valedictions ("Best regards," "Thank you for your consideration")
- No subject lines in talk/discussion posts

### No pre-placed templates or self-declined submissions.

## SECTION 6: CITATION & SOURCE RULES

### No UTM parameters in URLs:
- Strip `?utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `?utm_source=openai`, `?utm_source=copilot.com`, `?referrer=grok.com`

### No hallucinated reference markers:
- No `:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}`
- No `oai_citation`, `turn0search0`, `【85†L261-269】`
- No `attribution":{"attributableIndex":"X-Y"}`
- No `:::writing{variant="document"...`
- No `grok_card` / `grok_render_citation_card_json`
- No `[cite: 1]`, `[cite: 3, 12, 13]`
- No `↩` footnote markers
- No `[attached_file:1]`, `[web:1]`

### No book citations without page numbers.
### No broken external links. Verify every link.
### No invented DOIs or ISBNs.
### All citations must be real and verified.

## SECTION 7: STYLE & TONE

- Use **American English or British English consistently** — don't mix.
- Vary sentence length. LLMs default to medium-length, well-balanced sentences.
- Use **contractions** where natural: `don't`, `it's`, `can't`, `won't`.
- Use **idioms and colloquialisms** naturally (but don't force them).
- Allow **minor imperfections**: sentence fragments, parenthetical asides, informal transitions.
- Vary paragraph length. Some one-sentence paragraphs are fine.
- Don't end every paragraph with a tidy summary that ties back to the topic.
- Be specific rather than general. "Inventor of the first train-coupling device" beats "a revolutionary titan of industry."
